Ethereum can outperform Bitcoin while both lose money. Bitcoin can rise while Ethereum falls. These are different market observations, and saying only that one coin is stronger can hide the part that matters to a reader holding either asset.

## Separate direction from relative performance

These examples are hypothetical, not current quotes, forecasts or realized trading results.

| Completed session | Bitcoin return | Ethereum return | What the prices show |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| A | +2.00% | +3.00% | Both rose; Ethereum gained more |
| B | −4.00% | −2.00% | Both fell; Ethereum lost less |
| C | +1.00% | −1.00% | The coins moved in different directions |

In session B, Ethereum outperformed by **2 percentage points**, but a dollar-denominated Ethereum holding still fell in value. Relative outperformance and an absolute gain are not interchangeable.

Neither Bitcoin nor Ethereum stands for every crypto asset. Their networks have different designs and uses; [Ethereum's own comparison with Bitcoin](https://ethereum.org/ethereum-vs-bitcoin) explains those differences. A two-coin comparison cannot tell you what all smaller tokens, decentralized-finance markets or stablecoins are doing.

## What ETH/BTC adds

ETH/BTC expresses the price of one ether in bitcoin. Imagine Bitcoin is $100,000 and ether is $3,000: the ratio is `3,000 / 100,000 = 0.0300 BTC per ETH`.

If Bitcoin gains 2% and ether gains 3%, their new prices are $102,000 and $3,090. The ratio becomes approximately `0.030294`. The percentage change in the ratio is:

`(1.03 / 1.02 − 1) × 100 = approximately +0.98%`.

That is slightly different from subtracting the two dollar returns, which gives **+1 percentage point**. The subtraction is a return spread; the ratio change is a relative-price return. Label which one you mean.

## Match the clock before comparing prices

Crypto trades around the clock. A daily move needs a defined session, such as midnight-to-midnight UTC. Compare Bitcoin and Ethereum using the same provider, quote currency, start and end times. A rolling 24-hour change shown at lunchtime is not the same interval as yesterday's completed UTC candle.

Be especially careful when adding a US-stock or dollar-index comparison. Those markets have their own trading hours and holidays. A weekend Bitcoin move alongside an unchanged Friday reference does not establish that the other market reacted to it.

## What to watch next without predicting it

After both coins rise, ask whether the next completed session shows strength in both or only one. After both fall, distinguish a shared recovery from one coin simply losing less. For a split session, watch whether they begin moving in the same direction.

For ETH/BTC, compare several matching sessions instead of naming a new trend from one candle. A rising ratio during a falling dollar market means Ethereum has held up better in that comparison; it does not mean either coin has become low risk.

## Avoid explanations the data cannot support

Prices alone do not measure new exchange deposits, stablecoin supply, fund flows or the number of people buying. Every executed trade has a buyer and a seller; a price move is not a direct count of fresh investors. Those topics need their own datasets, timestamps and definitions.

A useful briefing can state that Ethereum fell less than Bitcoin, show the matched returns, and explain the ratio calculation. It should not turn those observations into an unsupported claim that money is rotating into all altcoins or that tomorrow's rally is assured.

## Five checks for a crypto comparison

1. Are both candles complete and from the same UTC interval?
2. Did the assets gain or lose in the quote currency?
3. Is the reported comparison a percentage-point spread or an ETH/BTC return?
4. Does the observation persist beyond one session?
5. Is any story about flows or causes backed by a separate source?
